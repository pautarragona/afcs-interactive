<p class="lede">Trim an aircraft, take your hands off, and nudge it. You will notice two things. First a quick nod: the nose bobs and settles within a couple of seconds. Then, much later, a slow swing: over about a minute the aircraft gently dives, speeds up, climbs back and slows down again. These are the two longitudinal modes, the <em>short period</em> and the <em>phugoid</em>, and nearly every pitch controller is designed around one of them.</p>

By the end you should be able to:

- explain in plain words what pushes the aircraft back in each mode, and what slows the motion down;
- estimate the short-period frequency and damping from the stability derivatives;
- estimate the phugoid period and damping from airspeed and <M t="L/D" />, and say why it rarely needs its own controller.

<h2 class="sec" id="s1"><span class="n">5.1</span>Two motions on two clocks</h2>

A car has the same split. Hit a bump and the suspension bounces once or twice in under a second. Drive over a long hill and the whole car rises and falls over a minute. Same car, two motions, and they barely interfere because their clocks are so different.

For the aircraft, the linear model of lesson B has four states: speed change <M t="u" />, angle of attack <M t="\alpha" />, pitch rate <M t="q" /> and pitch attitude <M t="\theta" />. Its four eigenvalues come as two complex pairs, one fast and one slow, typically ten to a hundred times apart. That gap is why each mode can be understood, and controlled, on its own.

<h2 class="sec" id="s2"><span class="n">5.2</span>The short period: a quick nod</h2>

**What you feel.** You fly into a rising gust. The air suddenly hits the wing from slightly below: the angle of attack jumps and you feel a bump. Within a second the aircraft swings its nose round to point into the new airflow, overshoots a little, and settles. It all happens so fast that the speed has no time to change.

**What pushes it back: the weathervane.** The tail sits well behind the centre of gravity, like the feathers of a dart. When the angle of attack rises, the tail meets the air at a larger angle too, makes more lift, and is pushed up. Tail up means nose down. How strongly this happens per degree of angle of attack is the derivative <M t="M_\alpha" />, negative for a stable aircraft. Move the centre of gravity backwards and the lever arm of the tail shrinks, so the weathervane gets weaker.

**What calms it down: damping.** While the aircraft rotates, the tail sweeps up or down through the air, like moving your hand through water: the faster you move, the more it resists. That is <M t="M_q" />. There is a second, quieter effect. Extra angle of attack makes extra lift, which bends the flight path upwards towards the nose and so reduces the angle of attack by itself. That is <M t="Z_\alpha" />.

So the nose behaves like a mass on a spring with a shock absorber, and the car's suspension equations carry over almost one to one:

Three things to try:

- **Move the centre of gravity aft.** Slide the weathervane stiffness towards zero. The nod gets slower and lazier, and just past zero the nose no longer comes back at all: the aircraft is statically unstable, as found in lesson B.
- **Take the damping away.** Slide <M t="M_q" /> to zero. The nose keeps bobbing for longer, but it still settles, because <M t="Z_\alpha" /> alone provides some damping.
- **Pull and release.** Switch the disturbance. After the pull, the angle of attack returns to where it was, but the nose (blue) stays higher: the aircraft is now climbing. A short pull does not leave the aircraft "more tilted into the air", it leaves it on a new path.

The table turns into the short-period approximation, the same <M t="\omega_n" /> and <M t="\zeta" /> as in lesson 1:

<Equation n="5.1" tex="\omega_{sp}^2 = Z_\alpha M_q - M_\alpha, \qquad 2\zeta_{sp}\,\omega_{sp} = -(Z_\alpha + M_q)" />

It is the motion the pilot feels first when moving the stick, which is why lesson 7 is entirely about how it should feel.

<h2 class="sec" id="s3"><span class="n">5.3</span>The phugoid: a slow swing</h2>

**What you feel.** Almost nothing, which is the point. Trimmed and hands off but a little slow, the nose drops a fraction, the aircraft starts a gentle descent and picks up speed. The extra speed makes extra lift, so it starts to climb, loses speed, and the cycle repeats, about once a minute. Passengers rarely notice.

**What drives it: a skateboarder in a half-pipe.** Going down, height turns into speed; going up, speed turns back into height. The aircraft does the same with its energy. The angle of attack hardly changes during all this, so the nod of section 5.2 is not involved; what changes the lift is the speed, since lift grows with the square of speed.

**What calms it down: friction.** For the skateboarder that is the wheels; for the aircraft it is drag. A clean, efficient aircraft, with a high lift-to-drag ratio, loses little energy per swing and keeps swinging for a long time.

Ignoring drag and the short period, Lanchester's energy argument gives the period, and adding drag gives the damping:

<Equation n="5.2" tex="\omega_{ph} \approx \sqrt{2}\,\frac{g}{V}, \qquad \zeta_{ph} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,(L/D)}" />

At 100 m/s and <M t="L/D = 12" /> that is one swing every 45 s with a damping ratio of only 0.06: barely damped, but so slow that a pilot corrects it without thinking. That is why it rarely gets its own control loop; the altitude and speed holds of lesson 8 take care of it.

<h2 class="sec" id="s4"><span class="n">5.4</span>Why the two clocks matter for control</h2>

When you drive, you correct the steering every second and choose the route every few minutes, and the two never get in each other's way. Flight control is built the same way. Inner loops (pitch rate, attitude) work at short-period speed and treat the airspeed as constant. Outer loops (altitude, speed, flight path) work at phugoid speed and assume the inner loop has already done its job. Every autopilot in lesson 8 has this nested structure, and it works because of the gap between the two time axes you just saw.

<h2 class="sec" id="s5"><span class="n">5.5</span>In short</h2>

- **Short period:** a quick nod at constant speed. The weathervane <M t="M_\alpha" /> is the spring, <M t="M_q" /> and <M t="Z_\alpha" /> the shock absorber: <M t="\omega_{sp}^2 = Z_\alpha M_q - M_\alpha" />, <M t="2\zeta_{sp}\omega_{sp} = -(Z_\alpha + M_q)" />.
- **Phugoid:** a slow swing trading speed for height at constant angle of attack, damped by drag: <M t="\omega_{ph} \approx \sqrt2\,g/V" />, <M t="\zeta_{ph} \approx 1/(\sqrt2\,L/D)" />.
- The gap between the two clocks lets inner and outer loops be designed separately.
