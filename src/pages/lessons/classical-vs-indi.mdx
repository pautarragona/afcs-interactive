<p class="lede">Classical flight control, as in this course, designs fixed or scheduled gains on linear models. Incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion, INDI, takes a different route: instead of modelling everything that acts on the aircraft, it measures the resulting acceleration and only models how a control surface changes it. The approach has been researched extensively at TU Delft, among other places, and flown on aircraft from small drones to full-scale research aircraft.</p>

By the end you should be able to:

- explain the idea of dynamic inversion and why plain inversion needs an accurate model;
- derive the incremental control law and say which model information it still needs;
- describe, from the bench, where INDI beats a fixed-gain controller and where it does not.

<h2 class="sec" id="s1"><span class="n">12.1</span>Dynamic inversion</h2>

Write the pitch-rate dynamics as a model part plus the control effect:

<Equation n="12.1" tex="\dot q = f(x) + g(x)\,\delta" />

where <M t="f" /> collects everything the aircraft does by itself (damping, static stability, disturbances) and <M t="g" /> is the control effectiveness, <M t="M_\delta" /> in the linear case. If both were known exactly, choosing <M t="\delta = (\nu - f)/g" /> would make <M t="\dot q = \nu" />: the aircraft would behave like a pure integrator, and a simple outer law <M t="\nu = K_v(q_c - q)" /> would give a first-order response with time constant <M t="1/K_v" /> at every flight condition. The catch is that <M t="f" /> contains everything that is hard to model, including disturbances, and any error in it appears directly in the response.

<h2 class="sec" id="s2"><span class="n">12.2</span>The incremental step</h2>

INDI avoids modelling <M t="f" />. Over one short control step, the state hardly changes while the control surface can move quickly, so

<Equation n="12.2" tex="\dot q \approx \dot q_0 + g\,(\delta - \delta_0)" />

where <M t="\dot q_0" /> and <M t="\delta_0" /> are the current, measured pitch acceleration and surface position. Asking for <M t="\dot q = \nu" /> gives the INDI law:

<Equation n="12.3" tex="\delta_c = \delta_0 + \hat g^{-1}\left(\nu - \dot q_0\right)" />

Everything that <M t="f" /> contained is now inside the measurement <M t="\dot q_0" />. The controller needs a model only of the control effectiveness, written <M t="\hat g" />, plus good measurements of angular acceleration and of the actuator position.

<h2 class="sec" id="s3"><span class="n">12.3</span>What the bench shows</h2>

Both controllers are designed for the same nominal aircraft: the classical one is a PI on pitch-rate error, the INDI one uses (12.3) with <M t="K_v = 5" /> 1/s.

- **Disturbances.** With the pitching-moment disturbance on, INDI's pitch rate barely dips, while the PI loses noticeably more before its integrator catches up. INDI sees the disturbance immediately in <M t="\dot q_0" /> and cancels it in the next increment.
- **Wrong effectiveness, too high.** Set the true effectiveness above what the controllers assume. The PI's loop gain rises with it and its overshoot grows; INDI only takes larger increments than needed and still converges cleanly.
- **Wrong effectiveness, too low, with a slow actuator.** Now lower the ratio and slow the actuator down. INDI's increments are too small and arrive late, and it starts to overshoot as well. INDI is not model-free: it depends on <M t="\hat g" /> and on actuator dynamics being fast compared with the response it is asked for.

<h2 class="sec" id="s4"><span class="n">12.4</span>The practical price</h2>

The measurement that makes INDI robust is also its main practical difficulty. Angular acceleration is rarely measured directly; it is usually obtained by differentiating filtered gyro signals, which adds delay. To keep the increment consistent, the actuator position is passed through the same filter, so that <M t="\dot q_0" /> and <M t="\delta_0" /> describe the same moment in time. Sensor noise, filter delay and actuator rate limits (lesson 9) set how high <M t="K_v" /> can go.

<h2 class="sec" id="s5"><span class="n">12.5</span>Where this leaves classical control</h2>

INDI does not replace the ideas of this course; it relies on them. The outer law <M t="\nu = K_v(q_c - q)" /> is a classical controller on an integrator. Its gain is limited by the same phase-margin reasoning as in lesson 4, with actuator and filter lags in the loop. Handling qualities, autopilot structure and the time-scale separation of lesson 8 all still apply. What changes is how much of the aircraft has to be modelled, and how gracefully the loop copes when the model or the aircraft changes.

<h2 class="sec" id="s6"><span class="n">12.6</span>In short</h2>

- Dynamic inversion cancels the aircraft's dynamics, but needs an accurate model of them.
- INDI replaces that model with measured acceleration: <M t="\delta_c = \delta_0 + \hat g^{-1}(\nu - \dot q_0)" />.
- It rejects disturbances and unmodelled dynamics well, and tolerates an overestimated effectiveness gracefully.
- It still depends on the effectiveness estimate, fast actuators, and well-synchronised acceleration and position measurements.
